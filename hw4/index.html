Overview

In this homework, I implemented a full cloth simulation pipeline using a mass-spring model. I started by representing the cloth as a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs. I then simulated the cloth's motion by accumulating external and spring forces, updating point mass positions with Verlet integration, adding damping, and applying Provot constraints to prevent excessive spring stretching. After that, I implemented collision handling with spheres and planes, as well as cloth self-collision using spatial hashing to avoid checking every pair of point masses. Finally, I implemented several GLSL shaders, including diffuse shading, Blinn-Phong shading, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and mirror reflection. Overall, this assignment made me learn how physical simulation and rendering interact: changing parameters like spring constant, density, damping, and mesh resolution can significantly affect both the motion of the cloth and its final visual appearance.

Part 1: Masses and springs

For Part 1, I implemented Cloth::buildGrid by constructing an evenly spaced row-major grid of point masses and then adding the three types of spring constraints between them. Structural springs connect each point mass to its immediate left and upper neighbors, shearing springs connect diagonal neighbors, and bending springs connect point masses two positions apart horizontally or vertically. Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

In this part, I implemented the force accumulation, Verlet integration update, and Provot-style spring length correction in Cloth::simulate. First, I accumulated the external forces on each point mass using the given external accelerations and the mass of each point mass. Then, I added spring forces using Hooke's law, while skipping any spring type whose constraint was disabled and using a weaker spring constant for bending springs. After computing the total force on each point mass, I updated the positions of all unpinned masses using Verlet integration with damping. Finally, I applied the Provot 10% deformation constraint so that no spring could stretch to more than 110% of its rest length in a single timestep.

With the default parameters, the cloth falls naturally under gravity and settles into a smooth hanging shape between the pinned corners. Changing ks changes how strongly the cloth resists stretching. With a very low spring constant, the cloth behaves more loosely and sags more; with a very high spring constant, the cloth behaves more stiffly and stays tighter. Changing density affects how strongly gravity pulls the cloth downward relative to the spring forces. A higher density makes the cloth sag more noticeably, while a lower density keeps it more taut. Changing damping mainly affects how long the cloth oscillates before coming to rest: low damping preserves motion, while high damping produces a more subdued settling behavior.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

I implemented collision handling for both spheres and planes. For sphere collisions, I checked whether each point mass was inside or intersecting the sphere by comparing its distance from the sphere origin to the sphere radius. If the point mass was inside the sphere, I computed the tangent point on the sphere surface in the direction from the sphere origin to the point mass, then moved the point mass from its previous position toward that tangent point while scaling the correction by 1 - friction. For plane collisions, I checked whether a point mass crossed the plane during the previous timestep. If it did, I computed the intersection point with the plane, offset it slightly above the surface using the provided surface offset, and then applied a friction-scaled correction from the point mass's previous position.

For the sphere scene, I compared the final resting state of the cloth using three different spring constants. With ks = 500, the cloth behaves more softly and stretches more easily, so it falls farther around the sphere and forms deeper folds. With the default ks = 5000, the cloth drapes naturally over the sphere with a moderate amount of folding and stretching. With ks = 50000, the cloth is much stiffer, so it resists stretching and holds a tighter shape over the sphere instead of conforming as deeply. The plane collision also works as expected: the cloth falls onto the plane and then comes to rest on the surface instead of passing through it.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

In this part, I implemented cloth self-collision using spatial hashing. Instead of checking every pair of point masses, which would be too expensive, I divided 3D space into grid cells and hashed each point mass into a cell based on its position. At each timestep, I first rebuilt the spatial map, then for each point mass I only checked possible collisions with other point masses in the same spatial cell. If two distinct point masses were closer than 2 * thickness, I computed a correction vector that moved the current point mass away from the other mass until the pair was at least 2 * thickness apart. I averaged all correction vectors for that point mass and scaled the result by the number of simulation steps to avoid overly sudden position changes.

The sequence below shows the cloth falling and folding onto itself in scene/selfCollision.json. Early in the motion, the cloth begins to bend and approach itself. In the middle of the fall, the lower part of the cloth folds back onto the upper layers, but the layers remain separated instead of clipping through each other. Later, the cloth settles into a more compressed folded shape on the plane. Although it can remain slightly bouncy, the self-collision handling prevents the cloth from passing through itself.

I also varied the spring constant and density to study how they change the folding behavior. With low ks, the cloth is softer and more flexible, so it forms larger, looser folds. With high ks, the cloth is stiffer and preserves a more structured shape. With low density, the cloth falls less aggressively and produces a smoother folded shape. With high density, the cloth is heavier and compresses into tighter folds when it collides with itself and the plane.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small program that runs on the GPU and controls part of the rendering pipeline. In this assignment, the shader program is made from a vertex shader and a fragment shader. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and transforms vertex data, such as positions, normals, tangents, and UV coordinates, into the coordinate spaces needed for rendering. It also passes interpolated values to the fragment shader. The fragment shader then runs for each fragment or pixel and uses the interpolated values, lighting information, textures, and camera position to compute the final color. Together, vertex and fragment shaders let us create different material effects, such as diffuse shading, specular highlights, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and mirror reflections.

For Blinn-Phong shading, I computed the final color as the sum of ambient, diffuse, and specular lighting. The ambient term adds a constant base amount of light so that surfaces are not completely black even when they are not directly lit. The diffuse term depends on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction, so surfaces facing the light appear brighter. The specular term creates shiny highlights by comparing the surface normal with the halfway vector between the light direction and the view direction. When all three components are combined, the result is a material with both soft diffuse lighting and sharper reflective highlights.

For texture mapping, I modified the texture in the textures directory and used a custom image as the color texture. For bump mapping, I used a non-default texture as a height map to perturb the surface normals during shading, which changes lighting without moving the actual mesh geometry. For displacement mapping, I used the same height texture but also displaced vertices along their normals in the vertex shader, which changes the actual geometry and silhouette of the surface. The difference between bump mapping and displacement mapping is especially clear when changing the sphere mesh resolution: displacement mapping depends much more strongly on the mesh density. For the mirror shader, I computed the view direction from the fragment position to the camera, reflected it across the surface normal, and sampled the cubemap in that reflected direction to create an environment-mapped reflection effect.
