Overview

In this assignment, I built a path tracer step by step. I started with camera rays and primitive intersections, then added BVH acceleration, better direct lighting with importance sampling, indirect lighting, and finally adaptive sampling. The most interesting part for me was seeing how each change showed up directly in the renders: BVH made rendering much faster, importance sampling reduced noise a lot, and multiple bounces added effects like color bleeding and softer lighting that rasterization does not really capture.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Task 1:

Ray generation and primitive intersection pipeline: Ray generation and primitive intersection are the first main steps of the rendering pipeline. For each pixel in the image, the renderer generates camera rays that pass through that pixel and into the scene. To implement this myself, I first took a sample point inside the pixel, converted it into normalized image coordinates, and mapped that point onto the virtual sensor plane in camera space. I made sure that in camera space, the camera is placed at the origin and looks down the negative z-axis, so the ray starts at the camera origin and points toward the corresponding point on the sensor plane. I then transform this ray from camera space into world space using the camera-to-world matrix. Once the ray is in world space, the renderer checks whether it intersects any primitives in the scene, such as triangles or spheres. If an intersection occurs, the renderer stores information about the closest hit including the distance along the ray, the surface normal, and the material at that point. This is what allows the renderer to determine which object is visible through each sampled pixel.

How I implemented ray generation and primitive intersection pipeline: In Camera::generate_ray(), I map normalized image coordinates onto the sensor plane at z = -1. I do this by scaling the x and y coordinates using the horizontal and vertical field of view to get the corresponding point on the virtual sensor in camera space. I then construct a direction vector from the camera origin to that sensor point and normalize it. Afterwards, I transform the ray direction into world space using the camera-to-world rotation matrix and use the camera position as the ray origin. I also initialize the ray’s min_t and max_t values using the near and far clipping planes. In raytrace_pixel, I repeatedly sample points inside a pixel, generate a ray for each sample, trace it through the scene, and take the average of the radiance values. For primitive intersections, I implemented separate intersection tests for triangles and spheres. In both cases, I check whether the ray hits the primitive at a valid t value between min_t and max_t, and when a closer hit is found I update max_t so that farther intersections are ignored.

Triangle intersection algorithm: For triangle intersection, I used the Möller-Trumbore algorithm. This algorithm finds the intersection between a ray and a triangle by solving for the ray parameter t and the barycentric coordinates of the hit point. The barycentric coordinates tell us whether the intersection point lies inside the triangle. If the computed coordinates satisfy the conditions that both values are nonnegative and their sum is at most 1, then the point is inside the triangle. I also check that the intersection happens within the valid range of the ray. If all of these conditions are satisfied, then the ray intersects the triangle.

How I implemented the triangle intersection algorithm: In Triangle::has_intersection, I first compute the two edge vectors of the triangle and then use cross products and dot products from the Möller-Trumbore algorithm to solve for the intersection. This gives me the barycentric coordinates u and v, along with the ray parameter t. I then check whether u and v describe a point inside the triangle and whether t lies within the valid range of the ray. If so, I return that an intersection exists. In Triangle::intersect, I use the same computation, but I additionally record the intersection information. I store the hit time, update the ray’s max_t so that farther hits can be ignored, and compute the surface normal using barycentric interpolation of the triangle’s three vertex normals. This gives smooth shading across the triangle instead of flat shading.

Sphere intersection algorithm: For sphere intersection, I used the standard ray-sphere intersection equation. By substituting the ray equation into the implicit equation of the sphere, I get a quadratic equation in t. The discriminant tells me whether the ray intersects the sphere at all. If the discriminant is negative, then there is no intersection. Otherwise, there are up to two possible intersection values, and I choose the closest valid one that lies between min_t and max_t.

How I implemented the sphere intersection algorithm: In Sphere::test, I compute the quadratic coefficients using the ray origin, ray direction, sphere center, and sphere radius. I then compute the discriminant and return false if it is negative. If the discriminant is nonnegative, I solve for the two roots and store the smaller one in t1 and the larger one in t2. In Sphere::has_intersection, I check whether either of these values lies within the valid interval of the ray. In Sphere::intersect, I choose the closest valid root, update max_t, and fill in the intersection structure. To compute the surface normal, I take the vector from the sphere center to the hit point and normalize it, which gives the correct smooth normal direction across the sphere.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Task 2:

BVH construction algorithm: I constructed the BVH recursively. For each node, I first computed the bounding box enclosing all primitives in the current iterator range. If the number of primitives was at most max_leaf_size, I made the node a leaf and stored the start and end iterators for that primitive subset. Otherwise, I computed a centroid bounding box over the primitives’ bounding-box centroids and chose the split axis as the axis with the largest extent. I then split primitives at the midpoint of that centroid bounding box along the chosen axis using std::partition, sending primitives with centroid coordinate less than the split coordinate to the left child and the rest to the right child. To avoid degenerate recursion when all primitives fall on one side, I fell back to splitting the range evenly at the midpoint of the iterator interval. This produces a binary hierarchy whose nodes bound spatially localized primitive subsets and allows rays to quickly reject large portions of the scene.

Rendering time comparisons: BVH acceleration produced a dramatic speedup over brute-force primitive intersection. On moderately complex scenes, the renderer without BVH had to test every ray against every primitive, causing render times to grow quickly with scene complexity. After enabling BVH traversal, rays first intersect node bounding boxes and only test primitives inside potentially hit leaves, which greatly reduces the number of primitive intersection tests. For example, cow.dae decreased from 43s without BVH to 0.2s with BVH, maxplanck.dae decreased from 62s to 0.3s, and CBlucy.dae decreased from 3 minutes to 1.4s. The speedup becomes more pronounced as scene complexity increases, showing that BVH is especially effective for dense triangle meshes.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Task 3:

Uniform hemisphere sampling implementation: For uniform hemisphere sampling, I estimated direct lighting at each surface intersection by randomly sampling directions uniformly over the hemisphere above the hit point. I first constructed a local coordinate frame whose z-axis aligns with the surface normal, so incoming sample directions could be generated in object space with the normal pointing upward. For each sampled direction, I transformed it into world space and cast a shadow ray from the hit point with a small epsilon offset to avoid self-intersection. If that ray intersected an emissive surface, I treated the emission of that surface as incoming radiance from that direction. I then accumulated the Monte Carlo estimator for reflected radiance using the diffuse BSDF, the cosine term, and the uniform hemisphere pdf of 1/(2π), and averaged over all samples. This method is unbiased, but because it samples the entire hemisphere without regard to where the lights actually are, most sampled directions do not hit the light source and therefore contribute little or no illumination.

Importance sampling implementation: For light importance sampling, I estimated direct lighting by sampling the scene’s light sources directly instead of sampling arbitrary hemisphere directions. At each hit point, I again constructed a local coordinate frame and computed the outgoing direction in object space. I then looped over every light in the scene and sampled directions toward that light using sample_L, which provides the incoming radiance, sampled direction, distance to the light, and the corresponding pdf. For point lights, I used one sample, while for area lights I used ns_area_light samples. After converting the sampled light direction into object space, I skipped samples behind the surface and cast a shadow ray toward the light with min_t = EPS_F and max_t = distToLight - EPS_F so that only blockers between the surface and the light would count as occlusion. If the shadow ray was unoccluded, I accumulated the reflected contribution using the BSDF, cosine term, and light-sampling pdf, then averaged across samples for each light and summed the contributions over all lights. Because this method concentrates samples directly on the actual light sources, it uses samples much more efficiently and converges far faster than uniform hemisphere sampling.

Comparison between uniform hemisphere sampling and importance sampling: On CBbunny, the difference between uniform hemisphere sampling and light sampling was obvious. With uniform hemisphere sampling, the image stayed noisy almost everywhere because most rays never hit the ceiling light. The bunny, walls, and floor were all covered in speckles. With light sampling, the image cleaned up much faster because the samples were aimed at the actual light source. The shadow under the bunny was still the noisiest part, but the rest of the scene looked much more stable.

Noise level comparison for 1, 4, 16, and 64 light rays: Using light importance sampling with one sample per pixel, increasing the number of light rays per shading point produced a clear reduction in noise throughout the scene, especially in the soft shadow beneath the bunny and on the back wall. With 1 light ray, the image was very noisy, with strong speckling across the bunny, floor, and walls, and the penumbra under the bunny was poorly defined. At 4 light rays, the noise decreased substantially and the overall shadow shape became much clearer, though noticeable variance still remained on the back wall and floor. At 16 light rays, the image became more stable, with smoother shading on the walls and a more consistent soft shadow region beneath the bunny. At 64 light rays, the render was the cleanest of the four, with the remaining variance reduced further and the shadow boundaries appearing the smoothest overall. These results show that increasing the number of light samples significantly improves direct lighting quality, with the largest visual gains occurring at lower sample counts and more gradual improvements as the number of light rays continues to increase.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Task 4:

Implementation of indirect lighting function: To implement indirect lighting, I extended the radiance estimator so that after computing direct illumination at the current intersection, it also recursively traces one additional bounce sampled from the surface BSDF. At each hit point, I first construct the local shading coordinate frame and compute the outgoing direction in object space. I then use bsdf->sample_f to sample an incoming direction, obtain the BSDF value and PDF for that sample, transform the sampled direction back into world space, and trace a new ray from the hit point with an epsilon offset to avoid self-intersection. If the new ray intersects the scene, I recursively evaluate the radiance arriving at the next hit point and weight it by the BSDF, the cosine term, and the inverse PDF. When accumulated bounces are enabled, I add the direct one-bounce illumination at the current point as well as the recursively estimated indirect contribution, which produces full global illumination.

Comparison of direct vs indirect illumination: Comparing the direct-only and indirect-only renders on CBspheres_lambertian.dae, the direct-only image captures the primary illumination from the ceiling area light and clearly defines the basic shape of the spheres, walls, and floor. The brightest regions are the surfaces that directly face the light, while the lower portions of the spheres and the shadowed regions remain very dark. In contrast, the indirect-only image is much dimmer and significantly noisier, since all of the illumination comes from bounced light rather than direct visibility to the source. However, this image reveals the global illumination effects that are missing from the direct-only result, especially the red and blue color bleeding from the side walls onto the spheres and nearby surfaces. Overall, the direct-only render provides the main shading structure of the scene, while the indirect-only render contributes the softer interreflection and color transfer that make the full global illumination result look more realistic.

Comparing bounce renders: Comparing the unaccumulated bounce renders for CBbunny.dae, the progression from m=0 through m=5 clearly shows how light transport builds up through the scene. At m=0, only the emissive ceiling light is visible, since no reflected light has yet contributed to the image. At m=1, the scene receives only direct illumination, which defines the overall shape of the bunny and produces the main shadow beneath it. The 2nd bounce is where indirect lighting first becomes clearly visible: light reflected from the walls and floor begins to illuminate regions that were dark in the direct-only image, and subtle red and blue color bleeding starts to appear on the bunny and surrounding surfaces. By the 3rd bounce, this indirect illumination spreads more evenly through the Cornell box, softening transitions between lit and shadowed regions and making the scene feel less harsh overall. Compared to rasterization, these 2nd and 3rd bounce effects are especially important because they capture interreflection and color bleeding that rasterization does not naturally model, which makes the rendered result look much more realistic and physically plausible.

Comparison of accumulated and unaccumulated bounce renders: Comparing the accumulated and unaccumulated bounce renders for CBbunny.dae shows the difference between isolating a single bounce depth and summing all light contributions up to that depth. In the unaccumulated images, each render displays only the contribution from one specific bounce, so the progression makes it easier to see how light moves through the scene: the 0th bounce contains only the emissive ceiling light, the 1st bounce gives the direct illumination, and the 2nd and 3rd bounces introduce softer indirect lighting and color bleeding from the red and blue walls. In contrast, the accumulated images combine all bounces from 0 through m, so each increase in m makes the final render more complete rather than replacing the previous contribution. The biggest visual jump happens from m=1 to m=2, where indirect light first fills in dark regions and adds more realistic interreflection. Beyond that, additional bounces continue to improve the image, but the changes become progressively smaller. Overall, the unaccumulated renders are useful for understanding how individual bounce depths contribute, while the accumulated renders show how these contributions combine to produce the final globally illuminated image.

Russian Roulette rendering comparison: In the Russian Roulette renders, the main visual change happens in the first few bounce depths. At m=0 only the light is visible, and at m=1 the scene has direct lighting but still looks harsh. By m=2 and m=3, bounced light starts filling in darker areas and the image looks much closer to the final result. After that, the difference gets pretty small. The m=100 image looks very similar to m=4, which matches the idea that most of the important light contribution comes from the first few bounces.

Comparing rendered views with various sample-per-pixel rates: Comparing renders of CBspheres_lambertian.dae at 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 64, and 1024 samples per pixel shows the expected reduction in Monte Carlo noise as the sample count increases. At 1 and 2 samples per pixel, the image is extremely noisy across nearly the entire scene, including the walls, spheres, floor, and shadow regions. At 4 and 8 samples per pixel, the overall structure becomes easier to read, but there is still substantial variance, especially in the indirectly lit regions and around the soft shadows beneath the spheres. By 16 and 64 samples per pixel, the render becomes noticeably more stable, with cleaner wall shading and more visible color bleeding from the red and blue side walls. At 1024 samples per pixel, the image is significantly smoother overall, and subtle global illumination effects are much clearer. Overall, increasing the number of samples per pixel steadily improves convergence by reducing variance throughout the image.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Task 5:

Adaptive sampling: Adaptive sampling is meant to avoid oversampling pixels that already look converged. Instead of giving every pixel the same large number of samples, it checks pixels as it goes and uses more samples only in the noisier parts of the image. In my implementation, I modified PathTracer::raytrace_pixel() to keep a running sum of illuminance values and squared illuminance values for each pixel. Every samplesPerBatch samples, I used those values to compute the sample mean and variance, then estimated a 95% confidence interval. If I ≤ maxTolerance·μ, I considered the pixel converged and stopped taking more samples there. Otherwise, sampling continued until the pixel converged or reached the maximum number of samples. I also stored how many samples each pixel actually used in sampleCountBuffer so I could generate the sampling-rate visualization.

Adaptive sampling results: The adaptive sampling images show that the renderer spent more samples on the parts of the image that were harder to converge. In the bunny scene, that mostly happened around the bunny outline, the ears, the shadow under the body, and near the ceiling light. The flat walls and floor needed fewer samples because their lighting changed more smoothly. I saw a similar pattern in the spheres scene, where the object boundaries and shadow edges got higher sample counts than the large flat wall regions.
